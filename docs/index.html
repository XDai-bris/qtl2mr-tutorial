Opening · 4 min · 00–04

Two traits move together. Which drives which?

An association is not a cause. Genetics can help us tell the two apart.

In your mice, animals with more X tend to have more Y. Three stories fit that pattern: X raises Y; Y raises X; or a third factor, U, raises both. An association mixes all three: cause, shared causes and reverse direction. We call the possible cause, X, the exposure, and the trait it may change, Y, the outcome.

Confounding is biological, not only environmental. A shared cause such as U is called a confounder: it makes X and Y move together even when X has no effect on Y. In human studies, confounders such as lifestyle and social circumstances can be adjusted for only if they are known and measured well; what is left over is called residual confounding. A mouse experiment does better on the environment, because diet, housing, age and handling can be held fixed. But holding the environment fixed is not the same as randomizing X. X still differs from mouse to mouse for biological reasons, such as a shared pathway, a hormone level or a physiological state, and some of these also act on Y. Most of them are unknown or unmeasured, so they cannot be adjusted for. In our simulated world, U is such a biological confounder.

Mendelian randomization (MR) uses the random inheritance of alleles to isolate the causal part. Which allele a mouse inherits is decided at random when its parents pass on their chromosomes, and it is fixed at conception, so neither X nor Y can change it. If a genetic variant G changes X, the mice that inherited the X-raising allele received, in effect, a small randomly assigned dose of X. If those mice also differ in Y, the difference travelled through X, provided three assumptions hold.

  1. 1G affects X: the variant really changes the exposure.
  2. 2G is independent of U: the hidden factors that move both traits do not decide which allele a mouse inherits, and do not share a cause with G, for example a gene that affects U and sits next to G on the chromosome.
  3. 3G affects Y only through X: there is no other path from the variant to the outcome.
More detailWords used in this guide
Exposure and outcome
X, the possible cause, is the exposure; Y, the trait it may change, is the outcome.
Confounder
A shared cause of the exposure and the outcome, such as U. It makes the two traits move together even when one has no effect on the other.
Instrument
A genetic variant used like G in the diagram. In DO mice our instruments are QTL for the exposure.
Founders
DO mice descend from eight inbred founder strains, labelled A to H. At each locus, each chromosome copy comes from one founder.
LOD score
The evidence from a QTL scan that a marker is associated with a trait; higher means stronger.
F statistic
How strong the founder signal for the exposure is at one locus: the joint test of the eight founder effects, averaged over its 7 degrees of freedom (the formula is in Part 1B; the code prints it as F_X_block). Larger means stronger; it does not show that an instrument is valid.
Kinship
How related two mice are. qtl2mr uses it to allow for relatedness, as qtl2 does in QTL scans.
95% interval
The range of values compatible with the data under the model. The intervals in this guide are nominal: they assume the model is right, and their coverage was not checked in this example.

Part 0 · Meet the simulated world · 3 min · 04–07

Meet the simulated world

We built this world, so we know the right answer and can check every method against it.

  • X → Y: effect 0.5. One unit more X causes half a unit more Y.
  • U raises both X and Y. U is hidden: it is not in the data.
  • Y has no effect on X.
  • QTL for Y act on Y directly; Part 1 uses them for the reverse question.

Genotypes were simulated with simcross (Karl Broman; CRAN), and traits with qtl2founderSim. We simulated one DO population, generated X and Y for 1,600 of its mice under the same truth, and then split those mice at random into three cohorts that share no mouse. The cohorts are three samples of the same population: they differ only in size and in which trait was measured.

More detailHow this world was built

X is controlled by 10 QTL, each with its own founder pattern: five split the founders into two groups, like a SNP, and five have three or more levels. Y has 4 QTL of its own, on other chromosomes. A hidden factor U raises both X and Y, X raises Y by 0.5, and the QTL for X affect Y only through X. The population was simulated with simcross's DO breeding design (20 generations of outbreeding from the eight founders); the 1,600 study mice were drawn at random from the 1,728 mice of its last generation, and X and Y were generated for all of them before they were split into the three cohorts.

The scans in data/ were computed in advance with qtl2 (scan1(), with leave-one-chromosome-out kinship). Simplifications: founder probabilities are exact (in real data they are estimated from SNP genotypes and are less clean), with no genotyping error, no covariates and no X chromosome.

A simulated world was accepted only if pre-set design checks passed (enough copies of every founder allele, strong enough QTL for X and for Y, an association of the expected size). Within this draw, no MR result was used to choose among the worlds tried. The cohort sizes were changed once, after an earlier draw gave a two-sample interval that just missed 0.5; all settings, including the seeds, were then fixed before these data were drawn (see data/simulation/README.md).

The code. The script that made these data is in data/simulation/, with a README that explains, step by step, how simcross and qtl2founderSim were combined, and why the association is expected to be about 1 when the true effect of X on Y is 0.5 and Y has no effect on X. You do not need to run it for the workshop.

Part 1 · 14 min · 07–21

Correlation is not causation

In Cohort 1 the association between X and Y is well above the causal effect; MR recovers the effect and its direction.

1AThe association

Fit a straight line of Y against X (a regression) in the 400 mice of Cohort 1. The slope says how much Y changes, on average, with one unit more X.

1BMR from X to Y

Choose instruments. Instruments are QTL for X. The scan for X was run in advance with qtl2. We keep the top marker of each region with LOD ≥ 3, and only if its F statistic is at least 4. The F statistic measures how strong the founder signal for X is at this locus: the joint test of the eight founder effects, averaged over its 7 degrees of freedom.

Estimate. At each instrument, qtl2mr estimates the eight founder effects on X and on Y, then finds the one slope that best links the Y pattern to the X pattern across all instruments. That slope is the MR estimate.

R · Task 03B · tasks/03_correlation_vs_causation.R
# PART B | MR from X to Y
cat("\nTASK 03B | MR from X to Y in Cohort 1\n")

# Step 1. Collect Cohort 1's qtl2 inputs in one object: founder probabilities,
# kinship (one matrix per left-out chromosome), traits and the marker map.
resources1 <- qtl2mr_prepare_resources(
  allele_probs = cohort1$genoprobs, kinship = cohort1$kinship,
  pheno = cohort1$pheno, covar = NULL, marker_map = cohort1$map

# of each region with LOD >= 3, kept only if its F statistic is at least 4.
instruments_x <- qtl2mr_select_instruments(
  resources1, cohort1$scan[, "X", drop = FALSE], exposures = "X",
  lod_thresh = 3, F_X_min = 4, progress = FALSE
)

# Show the instruments that passed both rules (F_X_block is the F statistic).
show_table(instruments_x[instruments_x$selected, c("marker", "chr", "pos", "lod", "F_X_block")])

# Step 3. Run MR from X to Y with these instruments.
# standardize_phenotypes = FALSE keeps X and Y in their own units, so the
# answer can be compared with the truth, 0.5. The slower optional analyses
# (bootstrap, leave-one-out, sensitivity, reports) are switched off.
mr_xy <- run_qtl2mr_analysis(
  qtl2mr_pair_input(resources1, instruments_x, exposure = "X", outcome = "Y"),

More detailThe F statistic, the idea behind the MR slope, the settings, and a chance instrument

The F statistic

F = b̂T Σ̂−1 b̂ / 7

b̂ holds the 7 founder contrasts for X at the locus (eight founder effects, compared with each other, give seven independent numbers), and Σ̂ is their covariance matrix: their squared standard errors, and how their errors move together. If the 7 contrast estimates were uncorrelated, F would be the average of their 7 squared z-scores (each estimate divided by its standard error). F ≥ 4 is this workshop's rule; qtl2mr's own guideline is F = 2, about nominal p = 0.05. The two rules play different parts: LOD ≥ 3 only finds candidate regions, and F ≥ 4 is the rule that decides. For a test with 7 degrees of freedom the F statistic is about two-thirds of the LOD score (F = 2 ln 10 × LOD / 7), so F ≥ 4 is roughly LOD ≥ 6.

The idea behind the MR slope

Take one instrument. Suppose inheriting founder C instead of an average founder raises X by 1 unit and Y by 0.5 units. If the instrument changes Y only through X, one unit of X causes 0.5 units of Y. qtl2mr uses all eight founder effects at each instrument, combines the instruments, and allows for the uncertainty of both the X and the Y effects.

The settings in the code

standardize_phenotypes = FALSE keeps X and Y in their own units, so the estimate can be compared with the truth, 0.5. The bootstrap, leave-one-out and instrument-sensitivity analyses are switched off to keep the run short; see the qtl2mr documentation before using these settings on your own data. The MR intervals are nominal 95% profile intervals.

One instrument is a chance finding

Because we built the world, we know where the true QTL are (they are listed in data/simulation/truth/true_qtl.csv). Eight of the 9 instruments sit on a true X-QTL. The ninth, chr11_042, has no true X-QTL nearby: the X-QTL on chromosome 11 lies 13.8 cM away, beyond the 10 cM we count as nearby, so it passed both rules by chance. The rules were fixed in advance and are not adjusted after seeing the results, so it stays. Two true X-QTL, on chromosomes 1 and 11, did not pass the rules in these 400 mice. Instruments chosen and used in the same mice can carry chance associations like this; that is one reason for the two-sample design in Part 2.

1CMR from Y to X, and the answers in both directions

Now the reverse question: does Y affect X? First, as in 1A, the regression the other way round: a straight line of X against Y. Then MR. Instruments for Y must change Y and must not act on X directly. QTL for X also move Y a little, through X, so a Y peak could sit on an X-QTL. As a simple rule, we drop any Y peak whose X LOD at the same marker is 3 or more.

What we see

  • The association of Y with X, 0.875, is inflated by U.
  • MR X → Y, 0.416, is near the truth of 0.5, which its interval (0.151 to 0.664) contains.
  • The regression the other way round, X on Y, gives 0.166 (95% interval 0.126 to 0.206), clearly above 0: on its own it could suggest that Y also drives X. Both slopes come from the same correlation between X and Y, so a regression cannot show direction. They are also in different units (X per unit of Y, not Y per unit of X), so they cannot be compared by size.
  • MR Y → X, 0.051 (95% interval −0.014 to 0.109), is close to 0: no sign that Y drives X, so the evidence points from X to Y.

MR gives evidence about cause only under the three assumptions in the diagram; here the simulation was built so that they hold for the true QTL (chance findings aside); with real data they have to be argued.

More detailA chance Y instrument, and what “close to 0” means

Four of the 5 Y instruments sit on the true QTL of Y. The fifth, chr7_015, has no true QTL nearby: it passed the fixed rules by chance and stays, as the rules require. Its X LOD, 2.697, was just under our cut-off of 3: the filter is a simple rule, not a perfect guard, and a marker with some X signal can slip through it. An interval around 0 does not prove that there is no effect; it says that any effect of Y on X is likely to be small, here between −0.014 and 0.109 under the model.

If Y did affect X, its true QTL would also show some X signal, so this filter can pull the Y → X estimate toward 0; it is a teaching simplification.

Part 2 · 12 min · 21–33

Two-sample MR

X and Y were never measured in the same mice, so no association can be computed. Yet MR estimates the effect.

Cohort A (500 mice) measured only X; Cohort B (700 mice) measured only Y. No mouse has both, so there is no X–Y association to compute at all. MR needs only two pieces for each instrument: its effect on X, from Cohort A, and the effect of the same locus on Y, from Cohort B. These effects can come from published founder-effect estimates, but only together with their full uncertainty: the covariance of the founder effects, in qtl2mr's contrast coding, which in practice you usually ask the authors for. Or they can be estimated with qtl2mr from founder probabilities and phenotypes on a shared marker map, as here. Two-sample MR assumes both cohorts come from the same population, so each locus has the same effect on X in both. Here they do: all three cohorts are samples of one simulated population.

2BTwo-sample MR

For each instrument, fit the eight founder effects on X in Cohort A and on Y in Cohort B. The small helper two_sample_block(), in R/mr_helpers.R, puts the two fits together and records that their errors are independent, because no mouse is in both cohorts. fit_multilocus_founder_mr() then finds the common slope.

R · Task 04B · tasks/04_two_sample.R
# PART B | Two-sample MR
cat("\nTASK 04B | X effects from Cohort A, Y effects from Cohort B\n")

# X measured in Cohort A and Y measured in Cohort B, named by mouse.
x_in_A <- setNames(cohortA$pheno$X, rownames(cohortA$pheno))
y_in_B <- setNames(cohortB$pheno$Y, rownames(cohortB$pheno))

# For each instrument: fit the founder effects on X in Cohort A, fit the
# founder effects on Y in Cohort B, then join the two fits into one block.
blocks <- lapply(markers, function(m) {
  # The chromosome of this marker, to pick the matching kinship matrix.
  chr <- cohortA$map$chr[cohortA$map$marker == m]

  # Founder effects on X, from the mice of Cohort A.
  fit_x <- fit_founder_lmm_block(cohortA$genoprobs, x_in_A,
                                 kinship = cohortA$kinship[[chr]], marker = m)

  # Founder effects on Y, from the mice of Cohort B.
  fit_y <- fit_founder_lmm_block(cohortB$genoprobs, y_in_B,
                                 kinship = cohortB$kinship[[chr]], marker = m)

  # Join the two fits; the helper records that their errors are independent.
  two_sample_block(fit_x, fit_y)
})

# Find the one slope that best links the Y patterns to the X patterns.
# allow_cross_cov = FALSE: no mouse is in both cohorts.
mr_2s <- fit_multilocus_founder_mr(blocks, allow_cross_cov = FALSE,
                                   compute_independence = FALSE, heterogeneity_method = "chisq")

# Show the estimate and its 95% interval.
show_table(data.frame(n_loci = length(blocks), estimate = mr_2s$theta,
                      lower = mr_2s$profile_ci_lower, upper = mr_2s$profile_ci_upper,
                      interval = mr_2s$confidence_set_type))

More detailWhy not just draw a line through the points?

A line drawn by eye, or by simple regression, through these points would be flatter than the MR line. The X values are estimates with their own noise, and noise in X flattens a simple regression line (regression dilution). The MR estimator allows for the uncertainty in the X effects and is designed to avoid this flattening. That is why the figure shows the MR line and the truth, not a line fitted through the points.

Part 3 · 8 min · 33–41

Built on qtl2's founder model

qtl2mr starts where a qtl2 analysis ends: the same founder probabilities, kinship and scans, and the same eight-founder view of a QTL.

This part has no new code. It shows how the steps of Tasks 03 and 04 connect to a qtl2 analysis.

3AThe same founder model as qtl2

If you have run qtl2's scan1() and scan1coef(), qtl2mr will feel familiar. Your scan1() LOD scores go straight into qtl2mr_select_instruments() to find the instruments. At each instrument, qtl2mr fits the founder effects with the same kind of linear mixed model that scan1coef() fits with kinship: the trait depends on the eight founder probabilities at the marker, and a kinship term allows for relatedness. qtl2mr writes the eight founder effects as seven contrasts, and its optional check validate_qtl2_scan1coef() confirms, at a chosen marker, that it reproduces the coefficients and standard errors of scan1coef() run with the same seven contrasts and the same heritability.

3CA QTL is an eight-founder pattern

Like qtl2, qtl2mr treats a QTL as a pattern across the eight founders, not as two alleles. At one locus each founder's haplotype can carry its own effect, so a QTL can have up to eight levels. A single SNP splits the founders into just two groups. When founders with opposite effects fall into the same group, their effects cancel, and the SNP can miss much of a strong QTL. qtl2mr uses the whole founder pattern at every instrument: each point of the MR scatter in Part 2 was one instrument's full founder pattern on X, and how much of that same pattern appeared in Y.

Take-home · 4 min · 41–45

Take-home

Three points, one for each part.

  1. An association is not a causal effect: here it was well above the truth (0.875 against 0.5), while MR recovered the effect (0.416) and its direction, as evidence that holds under the three assumptions.
  2. Two-sample MR estimated the effect (0.423) from X measured in one cohort and Y in another, where no association could be computed at all.
  3. qtl2mr is built on qtl2's founder model: it starts from the founder probabilities, kinship and scans that qtl2 users already have, and reads each QTL as an eight-founder pattern, not two alleles, so it keeps founder information that a SNP coding can lose.

Further steps

This workshop used a world built so that the MR assumptions hold for the true QTL; real studies need more.

Pleiotropy
A QTL may change Y through a path that does not go through X, breaking assumption 3. In DO mice this is a real risk, because one haplotype block can carry several genes. qtl2mr already reports a heterogeneity test across instruments and founders and can drop one instrument at a time; methods that stay reliable under pleiotropy are our next step.
Multivariable MR (MVMR)
Two exposures often share QTL, for example two traits on one pathway. MVMR estimates the direct effect of each on Y while holding the other fixed. We are developing a founder-based MVMR for DO mice.
